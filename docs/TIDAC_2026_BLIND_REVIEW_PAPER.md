# A Mobile Application for Melanoma Screening Support and Follow-Up Using Machine Learning

## Abstract
Melanoma is an aggressive form of skin cancer in which delayed attention to suspicious lesions can worsen patient outcomes. Although machine learning has improved image-based skin lesion analysis, many mobile solutions still emphasize prediction alone and provide limited support for safe communication, guided image capture, and longitudinal follow-up. This paper presents a mobile melanoma screening-support prototype designed to bridge that gap. The system was developed using a Design Science Research approach and combines a Flutter mobile frontend, Firebase-based data services, and a MobileNetV2-based prediction pipeline trained on the HAM10000 dataset. The application supports authentication, medical safety acknowledgement, guided lesion capture, patient-linked history, follow-up comparison, and educational content in addition to risk-oriented prediction. On a held-out test set, the current model achieved 0.6687 accuracy, 0.7968 AUC, 0.2291 precision, and 0.8383 recall. These findings suggest that the prototype is more suitable for screening support than for definitive diagnosis. The main contribution of the work is the integration of machine learning, follow-up workflow, and cautious result communication within a single patient-centered mobile system.

## Keywords
melanoma screening, mobile health, machine learning, skin lesion analysis, follow-up support, ethical medical communication

## Introduction
Melanoma is one of the most dangerous forms of skin cancer because delayed identification of suspicious lesions may allow rapid disease progression and poorer prognosis [1], [2]. In practice, many individuals do not seek timely medical review when they first notice visible skin changes. Limited access to specialists, uncertainty about lesion severity, and anxiety around cancer-related decisions can all contribute to delay [3]-[5]. As a result, the period between first visual concern and professional consultation remains an important problem for both healthcare access and digital health design.

Recent advances in deep learning have shown strong potential for image-based medical analysis, including skin lesion classification [7]-[10]. At the same time, mobile devices provide a practical platform for self-monitoring, image capture, and patient education. However, the value of a mobile melanoma application depends on more than classification accuracy alone. Prior work on health applications shows that user-centered design, trust, and communication quality are essential for meaningful adoption [6], [11]. Many existing systems present a prediction without adequately supporting capture consistency, longitudinal record keeping, or psychologically safe guidance after the result is shown.

This limitation is particularly important in melanoma-related use cases. A mobile application that communicates risk with excessive certainty may encourage false reassurance or unnecessary alarm. In addition, many research datasets used to train lesion classifiers are dermoscopic rather than ordinary smartphone image collections, which creates a gap between experimental model performance and real-world use [8], [9]. Therefore, a practical mobile solution should frame prediction as screening support, guide the user through a structured workflow, and clearly direct concerning cases toward clinical review instead of claiming diagnostic authority.

This paper presents a mobile application developed around that principle. The prototype integrates guided scan capture, machine learning-based risk screening, patient-linked lesion history, comparison support for follow-up, educational content, and cautious non-diagnostic messaging. The research objective is to investigate how such a system can be designed and implemented as a coherent patient-centered workflow rather than as an isolated classifier. The contribution is not a clinically validated diagnostic tool, but a software engineering artifact that combines prediction, follow-up support, and ethical communication within a single mobile platform.

## Methodology
The study follows a Design Science Research approach because its central activity is the construction and evaluation of a software artifact intended to address a real-world problem. A pragmatic perspective was adopted in order to combine knowledge from melanoma screening literature, digital health communication, and machine learning implementation with the practical demands of mobile software engineering. The resulting artifact was designed as a screening-support prototype rather than as a regulated medical device.

The machine learning component was developed using the HAM10000 dataset, a widely used collection of dermoscopic lesion images [8]. For the current prototype, the original diagnostic labels were simplified into a binary classification task: melanoma and non-melanoma. This decision aligned the model output with the application goal of risk-oriented support instead of multi-class diagnostic interpretation. Images were resized to 224 x 224 and processed using a transfer learning pipeline based on MobileNetV2. Data augmentation included horizontal flipping, mild rotation, zoom, and contrast variation. To manage class imbalance and stabilize training, the workflow used class weighting, early stopping, checkpointing, and learning-rate reduction. The dataset was split using stratified training, validation, and test partitions, with validation and test ratios of 0.15 each.

The mobile application was implemented with Flutter and Dart. Firebase Authentication was integrated for account creation, sign-in, password reset, and session handling. Cloud Firestore was used to store structured scan metadata and patient-linked follow-up records, while Firebase Storage was used to store lesion images associated with the saved scans. A local FastAPI service exposes the trained model for prediction, and TensorFlow Lite export was retained for future on-device inference work. This separation of the mobile interaction layer and prediction layer allows the inference component to evolve independently from the user interface.

The operational workflow of the application was designed to reflect the practical steps of self-monitoring rather than only image upload and classification. After account access, the user completes onboarding and a one-time medical safety acknowledgement. The scan flow then supports patient selection, body-area identification, lesion labeling, a brief questionnaire, and image capture from the camera or gallery. For live capture, a short stabilization step was included to encourage more consistent framing. Once the prediction is returned, the system presents supportive categories such as low risk, caution advised, or specialist review recommended instead of a diagnostic statement. The result, image, and related metadata can then be saved for later review.

Longitudinal follow-up was treated as a core design requirement. The application groups saved records by patient, body area, and lesion label, enabling repeated review of the same lesion across time. A comparison feature and ghost-reference guidance were included to help users capture follow-up images more consistently. Educational screens covering ABCDE signs, melanoma stages, common questions, prevention guidance, and UV-related advice were also integrated so that the prototype would function as a broader screening-support environment rather than a single-purpose model interface.

Evaluation at the current stage focused on two areas. First, the held-out test set was used to assess predictive performance using accuracy, AUC, precision, recall, and loss. Second, the implemented workflow was reviewed as a functional prototype, with attention to whether the main end-to-end sequence from authentication to prediction, storage, history review, and follow-up support was operational. Because the present work is still at prototype stage, large-scale user testing, psychological impact analysis, and clinical validation were treated as future work rather than completed evaluation tasks.

## Results and Discussion
The current implementation demonstrates that the proposed concept can be realized as an integrated mobile system. The prototype already supports email and password authentication, onboarding, a one-time safety acknowledgement, patient-aware scan preparation, image capture or gallery selection, prediction through the local API, structured history storage, follow-up planning, lesion comparison, and educational guidance. This is an important progression beyond a basic classifier because the value of the system depends on how prediction is embedded within the full user journey.

The machine learning results from the held-out test set were 0.6687 accuracy, 0.7968 AUC, 0.2291 precision, 0.8383 recall, and 0.6531 loss. The high recall is encouraging for a screening-support scenario because suspicious melanoma-like cases are less likely to be missed than they would be with a low-recall model. However, the relatively low precision indicates that the classifier may generate a substantial number of false positives. This means the current model is more appropriate for cautious triage and self-monitoring support than for high-confidence diagnostic use. In other words, the model can help encourage follow-up attention, but it should not be treated as evidence of confirmed disease.

The system-level contribution of the prototype is stronger than the model metrics alone suggest. One of its major strengths is that prediction is not presented as an isolated endpoint. Before analysis, the user is guided to identify the lesion context through body-area selection, lesion labeling, and a short questionnaire. After analysis, the user can save the result, revisit the lesion history, compare earlier and later entries, and receive follow-up-oriented messaging. This workflow better reflects the realities of self-observation, where change over time and clarity of next steps are often more important than a single probability value.

Another notable strength is the deliberate use of cautious communication. Health applications can cause harm if they present uncertain results as clinical truth. The prototype therefore avoids direct diagnostic claims and instead frames output as screening support, using graded recommendations that encourage specialist review when appropriate. This design choice was informed by literature on communication of serious health information and by the broader need for emotionally responsible mobile health interfaces [3], [4], [11]. The inclusion of a one-time safety acknowledgement and educational content further supports this positioning.

The follow-up design also adds practical value. Grouping scan history by patient, body area, and lesion label creates a more meaningful longitudinal record than storing disconnected predictions. The comparison flow and ghost-reference overlay attempt to improve repeat-image consistency, which is relevant because lesion evolution is clinically meaningful and casual smartphone capture can otherwise vary substantially. From a software engineering perspective, these features help move the application beyond a one-step prediction tool toward a structured self-monitoring environment.

Despite these strengths, several limitations remain. The first is domain mismatch. HAM10000 is primarily a dermoscopic dataset, whereas the mobile application can accept ordinary smartphone images. As a result, acceptable dataset-based performance does not imply equivalent real-world clinical behavior [8]-[10]. The second limitation is the dependence on a local prediction API in the current implementation, which is suitable for research and testing but not yet for production deployment. The third limitation is the absence of formal usability and psychological impact studies. Since the application communicates health-related risk, its wording and interaction patterns should eventually be evaluated with real users and, ideally, with clinical input.

Overall, the results support the feasibility of the proposed approach. The prototype shows that machine learning-based melanoma screening support can be integrated with guided capture, longitudinal follow-up, cloud-backed history, and cautious communication in a single mobile workflow. At the same time, the observed precision limitations and the gap between dermoscopic training data and smartphone use reinforce the need to position the artifact as an academic screening-support prototype rather than a diagnostic product.

## Conclusion
This paper presented a mobile melanoma screening-support and follow-up application developed using machine learning, Flutter, Firebase, and a MobileNetV2-based prediction pipeline. The work addressed a gap in many existing solutions by combining prediction with guided capture, longitudinal record management, educational support, and ethically cautious communication. The prototype achieved encouraging recall and AUC on a held-out test set, while its lower precision confirmed that the system is better suited to screening support than to definitive diagnosis.

The current artifact demonstrates a useful direction for patient-centered mobile health software, especially in contexts where early awareness and structured follow-up are valuable. Future work should focus on smartphone-specific validation, broader usability testing, production-grade deployment of the inference service, and deeper clinical review of the communication and follow-up workflow. Within those boundaries, the present prototype provides a strong foundation for further research and refinement.
